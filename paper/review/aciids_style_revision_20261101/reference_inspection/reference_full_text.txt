        Abstract. Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) can use a graph to
        organise evidence. We ask whether its quality gains justify construction
        cost. We compare four structured systems (GraphRAG, LightRAG,
        RAPTOR and HippoRAG 2) with flat retrieval. We use a generator
        with eight billion parameters (8B), common chunks and four sampled
        question-answering benchmarks. Our token ceiling covers construction
        and 1 500 queries. Flat uses only 0.7 to 13.9% of that allowance. With
        original responses, HippoRAG 2 leads fixed flat by 6.2 and 15.6 token-
        F1 points (answer word overlap) on MuSiQue and 2WikiMultihopQA.
        When we select between two flat settings on separate calibration ques-
        tions, the gaps remain 6.2 and 13.6. Short-answer extraction raises fixed
        gaps to +7.4 and +17.6, but human judgments identify 21 meaning-
        changing extractions and two unclear cases among 76 outputs. Ligh-
        tRAG’s MuSiQue gain depends on extraction. Flat leads on Narra-
        tiveQA. QuALITY remains inconclusive under question and document
        resampling. A near tie moves RAPTOR’s cost intersection from about
        170 to 454 queries. We therefore check both scoring rules and quality
        tolerances before comparing costs. Our conclusions concern the tested
        configurations, which leave much of the flat budget unused.

        Keywords: Retrieval-augmented generation · Knowledge graphs · To-
        ken accounting · Evaluation methodology · Question answering.


1     Introduction

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) answers questions by giving a language
model, the reader, passages retrieved from a corpus. Flat retrieval searches these
passages directly, while structured approaches first organise them into graphs
or trees. On the graph side, GraphRAG [2] extracts an entity graph and sum-
marises connected groups, and LightRAG [4] combines entity and relation re-
trieval. RAPTOR [17] instead builds a passage-summary tree. HippoRAG 2

[5] also relies on a graph, which it uses to rank evidence. We call these methods
structured retrieval and compare them with our flat+ pipeline, which searches
corpus chunks without a graph or summary tree.
    Unlike flat+, these systems must build their structure before they answer
any question. This upfront cost may be recovered over later queries [5,6,19], but
whether the investment is worthwhile depends on answer quality and on the
total cost of building and querying the index. Under a common token ceiling
that counts construction and query tokens, we ask when structured retrieval
answers better than flat retrieval and how evaluation choices change the cost
comparison. We report actual consumption next to the ceiling because the flat
configurations we test leave much of their allowance unused.
    Figure 1 shows why the comparison must consider how the reader uses ev-
idence. Asked where Maurice of Orange’s father died, flat+ retrieves both
supporting passages among forty chunks but answers with his birthplace. Hip-
poRAG 2 answers correctly from five passages. Retrieving the right facts does
not guarantee the right answer. Across 2WikiMultihopQA (2Wiki), HippoRAG 2
beats flat forty by 17.6 F1 points after short-answer extraction (panel c). This
gap alone cannot show that graph connections improve evidence use, because the
systems also differ in context length, passage order and prompts. GraphRAG
offers two query modes. Local search answers from evidence linked to the enti-
ties in the question, whereas global search combines answers from community
reports. The figure shows local search, and Sect. 7 also reports global search.
    We study these tradeoffs with a single generator, Llama 3.1 8B Instruct [3],
under greedy decoding. Within each dataset, every system indexes the same
passages. We evaluate on MuSiQue [18] and 2Wiki [7] for multi-hop question
answering and on NarrativeQA [10] and QuALITY [14] for long-document un-

derstanding, with 293 to 300 sampled questions per benchmark. Besides the cost
audit, we test whether the advantages hold when we score original responses and
choose the flat configuration on separate calibration questions. We also check
whether short-answer extraction preserves meaning and whether a small change
in tolerated quality loss alters the flat comparator.
Our study makes three contributions.
 – Graph structure helps on multi-hop questions only. Scored on its original
   responses, HippoRAG 2 beats flat retrieval by 6.2 F1 points on MuSiQue
   and 15.6 on 2Wiki, and the margins stay at 6.2 and 13.6 when the flat
   setting is chosen on held-out calibration questions. Flat forty retrieves more
   MuSiQue support than HippoRAG 2 reads, which suggests that the gain
   lies in how evidence reaches the reader. On NarrativeQA, flat retrieval leads
   every structured system, and QuALITY remains inconclusive.
 – Answer post-processing can create a winner. Short-answer extraction lifts
   LightRAG’s MuSiQue F1 from 9.9 to 34.6 and reverses its comparison with
   flat retrieval. A human audit finds that extraction changed the meaning
   of 21 of 76 sampled outputs. Raw responses should therefore anchor the
   comparison, with extracted scores reported as secondary.
 – Break-even points depend on the stated quality tolerance. On NarrativeQA,
   RAPTOR and flat twenty differ by less than 0.004 F1 points. Strict match-
   ing forces the costlier flat forty as comparator and puts RAPTOR’s break-
   even at about 170 queries, whereas a 0.01-point tolerance admits flat twenty
   and moves it to 454. A cost claim means little without its tolerance.
These findings hold for the tested configurations and sampled corpora. They do
not show that graph connections cause the multi-hop gain.
   Section 2 reviews related work. Sections 3 and 4 define the accounting and
evaluation protocol, Sect. 5 gives an exploratory evidence heuristic, Sects. 6 and 7
report the experiments, and Sects. 8 and 9 conclude.

2   Related Work
We relate our study to structured retrieval, flat alternatives and the tasks used
to evaluate them.

Structured retrieval and its cost. HippoRAG 2 already reports construction to-
kens, indexing time, query time and memory. Han et al. [6] compare flat and
graph retrieval on question answering (QA) and summarization, including effi-
ciency and fixed retrieval-token analyses. Xiang et al. [19] examine construction,
retrieval and generation on graph-specific tasks. We build on these observations
by testing whether HippoRAG 2’s advantage persists across our flat settings
and scoring rules, and by examining how small quality differences affect cost
comparisons.
    Efficient indexing can also change the tradeoff. KET-RAG [8] reduces con-
struction cost with a selective graph skeleton and a structure linking text to key-
words. The contemporaneous EffiRAG preprint [22] proposes a system grounded

Flat retrieval and context use. flat+ combines BM25 word matching, dense
vector search, rank fusion and reranking [16,9,1,13]. Its static decomposition gen-
erates subquestions together. Dependent reasoning uses earlier answers to guide
later retrieval [15]. Long-context comparisons [11] suggest further uses of our un-
used flat budget. Context-position effects [12] and Zarrinkia et al.’s structured
prompting and compression [21] help explain why more retrieved evidence need
not improve answers. Testing these explanations in our setup requires controlled
comparisons.

Evaluation coverage. MuSiQue and 2WikiMultihopQA (2Wiki) annotate sup-
port for multi-hop questions [18,7]. NarrativeQA and QuALITY test understand-
ing of long documents [10,14]. These tasks allow direct reference-answer scoring.
Graph-specific resources cover hierarchical retrieval, summarization and domain
reasoning [19,20], which extend beyond our QA conclusions.


3    Construction and Query Accounting

We distinguish construction from work repeated per question, counting input
and output language-model (LM) tokens equally. Each token is a word or word
fragment processed or generated by the model. Table 1 gives cleaned recorded
costs: calls attributed to the final build after removing earlier builds and esti-
mated retries. Section 7.3 examines that cleanup.
    On MuSiQue, GraphRAG’s index uses 68.9 M tokens, enough for approx-
imately 11 622 queries with the largest tested flat+ setting. As we ask more
questions, each bears a smaller share of this initial cost. This is amortization.
We compare total tokens at a stated query volume and check quality separately.
Monetary cost and elapsed time require further measurements.

4     Protocol and Evidence Measures

We explain our token ceiling, flat selection rules and evidence measures. We
measure quality with token F1 , the harmonic mean of precision (the fraction of
answer words matching the reference) and recall (the fraction of reference words
covered). We distinguish these words from LM cost tokens and report mean F1 ,
or QuALITY option accuracy, on a 0 to 100 scale. Raw scoring uses original
responses with prefix/first-line cleanup (Sect. 6).


4.1   A common token ceiling

Let Π denote a system configuration and Q the expected number of questions
answered with one index. We define its total recorded token cost as

                       K(Π, Q) = Kidx (Π) + Q Kqry (Π),                        (1)

Here Kidx counts construction tokens and Kqry counts mean query tokens, includ-
ing subquestions, fact filtering and map-reduce (answering over separate reports
before combining responses). We pay construction once and queries Q times.
    At Q = 1 500, we set each benchmark’s ceiling to the largest graph-system
total. Our policy, fixed before scoring, selects the largest affordable count among
five, ten, twenty and forty. All fit, giving forty throughout. We report allowance
and consumption separately.

We recover the graph’s extra construction cost (the numerator) through its sav-
ing on each query (the denominator). Both must be positive. We require strict
quality matching, then allow losses of 0.01, 0.1, 0.5 and 1 point to see whether
the eligible flat setting changes. These tolerances do not establish statistical
equivalence.


4.2   The flat configuration family

combine their rankings through reciprocal-rank fusion, then rerank them with
a cross-encoder that scores question-passage pairs. The reader receives k = 5
chunks, or k ∈ {10, 20, 40} after at most three jointly generated subquestions
search independently. Each passage retains its best rerank score across the orig-
inal question and subquestions. These settings form our flat ladder.
    Our quality-cost frontier includes ablations that remove pipeline steps. It
connects settings with no tested alternative at least as accurate and no more
costly, with one strict improvement. We separately choose between k = 10 and

4.3      Minimum evidence, annotated support, and prefix success

We distinguish a conceptual evidence minimum from two observable measures.
                               A Token-Budget Audit of Graph-Based RAG             7

Definition 1 (Minimum evidence size). For question q with answer y, loc(q)
is the smallest size of a corpus chunk set sufficient to derive y under a fixed
inference criterion and allowed background knowledge.
Annotated support count g(q) counts benchmark supporting paragraphs. It
bounds loc(q) from above if they suffice. Figure 1 uses two passages, although
another sufficient set might be smaller.
     For our reader-dependent proxy h(q), we retrieve twenty candidates using the
question and gold answer, then test increasing prefixes for correctness. Success
means free-form F1 ≥ 0.5 on a 0 to 1 scale, or the correct QuALITY option.
The first m for which prefixes m and m + 1 succeed defines h(q): success at
two and three gives h(q) = 2. Only 1 ≤ m ≤ 19 can be certified. Other cases
are unresolved, stored as marker 21. Because either retrieval or the reader can
fail, we cannot infer how much evidence the question intrinsically needs. We
report unresolved rates and resolved-case medians, excluding these gold-assisted
diagnostic calls from system cost.


5   An Exploratory Evidence Heuristic
Each required passage creates another chance to miss evidence. Retrieving two
independently with probability 0.8 each gives joint success 0.82 = 0.64. We ask
whether a graph could gather complete evidence more reliably.
    We model one fixed set of ` chunks, all necessary and jointly sufficient, with no
alternative sufficient set. Let r ∈ (0, 1) be each chunk’s independent flat-retrieval
probability. Let rg ∈ [0, 1] be the probability of retrieving graph evidence. Let
 ∈ [0, 1] denote its conditional failure probability. Usable graph evidence then
has probability rg (1 − ). We hold this probability constant as ` changes. If both
readers answer correctly with probability a ∈ (0, 1] given sufficient evidence and
otherwise fail, flat correctness is ar` . Graph correctness is pg = arg (1 − ). For
pg > 0, the graph wins when

                                              log r
We derive this threshold in the supplement. Allowing alternative sufficient sets
would give flat retrieval more chances to succeed.
   Our exploratory estimate uses 100 disjoint calibration questions per multi-
hop dataset, substituting mean F1 on a 0 to 1 scale for a and pg , and annotated
support count for `. Our estimate is exploratory: dependent retrieval, varying
graph success or answers without all support can violate the untested probability
assumptions. We examine failed predictions in Sect. 7.


6   Experimental Setup
We specify the data, systems and scoring rules before describing token accounting
and statistical checks.
8      H.G.-H. Pham et al.

Data and splits. For MuSiQue and 2Wiki, we use the HippoRAG 2 releases and
seeded samples of 300 evaluation and 100 disjoint calibration questions. We in-
dex their pooled supporting and distractor paragraphs, yielding 5 723 and 2 679
passages. NarrativeQA uses the ten-document release (4 111 passages, 293 ques-
tions). For QuALITY, we pack validation articles into sentence-aligned passages
of at most 128 tokens (870 passages, 300 questions from 17 articles). Within each
dataset, all systems receive the same corpus.

Systems and shared components. GraphRAG 2.3.0 uses one gleaning round (an
extra extraction pass), Leiden community detection and level-two local/global
search. Local search retrieves entity-related evidence. Global search combines
community-report answers. LightRAG 1.3.9 uses one gleaning round and hy-
brid entity/relation search. Official RAPTOR uses five summary levels, 100-
token summaries and cross-level retrieval limited to ten nodes and 3 500 tokens.
HippoRAG 2 (2.0.0a4) extracts facts, links similar entities, filters facts through
recognition memory and retrieves five passages using personalised PageRank
(query-seeded graph ranking). All use Llama 3.1 8B Instruct with greedy de-
coding (highest-scoring next token) and bge-large-en-v1.5 embeddings. flat+
uses bge-reranker-base. Prompts and answer limits differ by implementation.
Our single run per configuration cannot establish pipeline determinism.

Scoring and extraction. We score token F1 and exact match (EM) after low-
ercasing, removing punctuation and articles, and normalizing whitespace. We
maximize over accepted references (gold aliases). For QuALITY accuracy, our
parser accepts a parenthesized letter, then a leading letter, then the closest op-
tion text. Free-form raw scoring removes a recognized answer prefix and keeps
the first answer line.
    For multi-hop responses exceeding eight whitespace-delimited words, we also
score the same model’s short extraction, labeled “normalized” in existing tables.
It receives the question and at most 4 000 response characters, with a 32-token
output limit and no passages or gold answers. We give prompts, EM and ab-
lations in the supplement and exclude extraction from deployment cost. One
author assessed 76 changed outputs, sampled deterministically with one per
nonempty dataset/system/score-change group (stratum). We omit gold answers
from the worksheet to focus judgments on preservation of meaning.

Token accounting. We log server-reported input/output usage by system, corpus
and phase. Query means include decomposition, filtering and report aggregation.
Construction counts successful final-build calls. We report embedding tokens sep-
arately without measuring embedding/reranking runtime or energy. Unrecorded
cancelled calls and intermediate retries leave some token consumption unknown.

Statistical analyses. Our 95% confidence intervals (CIs) span the 2.5th to 97.5th
percentiles of bootstrapped mean differences. We use 1 000 paired question draws
for original comparisons and 2 000, seed zero, for primary and sensitivity anal-
yses. For long documents, we resample documents with all their questions to

7.2   Flat configurations and calibration selection

We find that sending more passages to the reader raises cost without reliably
improving answers (Table 4). MuSiQue twenty scores 31.5 versus 30.9 at forty.
2Wiki ten scores 44.1 versus 42.1 at forty. We therefore check selection using
the two saved calibration candidates, keeping evaluation answers separate. The
selected raw gaps remain 6.2 and 13.6 (Table 2). Their extracted flat scores are
30.7 and 43.4, with gaps of 7.4 and 15.6. Our multi-hop finding survives this
check. Calibration outputs for five, twenty and long documents are unavailable.
    In Fig. 2, we show quality (0 to 100) against total LM tokens in millions
on a logarithmic axis. Higher and farther left is better. Multi-hop extraction
explains differences from Table 4. The dotted frontier has no measured interme-
diate points or policy for future questions. NarrativeQA global uses 100 questions
versus 293 elsewhere. We give matched scores in the supplement.
    We examine support recall, the fraction of annotated supporting passages
retrieved, to understand why more text helps so little. From dense retrieval to
flat forty, recall rises from 58.6 to 78.3 on MuSiQue and from 72.5 to 84.4 on
2Wiki, with little F1 improvement. Flat forty retrieves more MuSiQue support
than HippoRAG 2’s five passages. For our reader, coverage appears insufficient.
Differences in context length, order, prompts and graph connections prevent us
from isolating the cause.

7.3         Robustness of scores and accounting

We check extraction, parsing, question coverage, document dependence and ac-
counting cleanup against the saved runs.

Answer transformation. We examine extraction because it raises LightRAG’s
MuSiQue score from 9.9 to 34.6, reversing its comparison with flat. Our 1.90M
recorded tokens over 15 194 calls include calibration, unused long-document ex-
traction and repeated work. Without per-question identifiers, we cannot allocate
this total to changed evaluation answers.
    We asked one author to assess the 76 sampled outputs. The author labeled
53 as preserved, 13 as unsupported additions, seven as wrong selections, one as
answer loss, and two as unclear. For example, N035 asks which film was released
earlier. LightRAG states, “Jazz Boat was released in 1960, while Her Husband’s
Trademark was released in 1922.” The extractor returns “1922”, giving a year
where the question asks for a film. The year comes from the response, but ex-
traction loses the comparison needed to identify the film. We judge preservation
of meaning separately from reference-answer correctness.
    Our 76 outputs cover 56 unique dataset/question pairs. With one annotator
and one item per stratum, we cannot estimate agreement or within-stratum
12     H.G.-H. Pham et al.

variance. Confidence is high for 63 labels, medium for eight and low for five. We
report weighted counts and examples in the supplement without using labels to
repair test answers. Extraction adds processing whose effects vary by system.

Parser sensitivity. We test parsing sensitivity by retaining QuALITY’s letter
rules but assigning zero when no letter is recognized. GraphRAG global uses
option-text fallback on 101/300 answers, with 22 ties and 11 all-zero overlaps.
Its accuracy falls from 38.0 to 28.7. Flat forty stays at 61.0. HippoRAG 2 falls
from 63.3 to 63.0, with a paired gap CI of [−3.7, 7.7]. Its comparison with flat
remains inconclusive despite GraphRAG global’s parser-sensitive score.
    For free-form answers, we retain the full response after the same prefix. Our
HippoRAG 2 fixed-flat gaps remain 5.7 and 15.5 (5.7 and 13.5 after calibration).
We apply both alternatives uniformly, without case-specific repair. Neither is a
complete semantic judge: letter-only parsing can reject valid option text, while
extra explanation can lower F1 precision.

Matched questions and document dependence. We next align question coverage.
On 100 matched NarrativeQA questions, flat forty scores 26.0. Scores are 24.3 for
HippoRAG 2, 24.5 for RAPTOR, 14.4 for GraphRAG local, 6.5 for global and
4.9 for LightRAG. Flat’s lead persists. Across all 293 questions, the RAPTOR-
minus-flat document CI is [−5.3, −0.3]. Deleting any of the ten documents leaves
gaps between −3.8 and −2.4. QuALITY’s seventeen-article CI for HippoRAG 2
minus flat is [−2.0, 6.1]. Our sampling checks retain flat’s NarrativeQA lead and
leave QuALITY inconclusive, although both document samples are small.

Cleanup sensitivity. To check cleanup sensitivity, we count all recorded index-
ing calls. NarrativeQA GraphRAG rises from 37.17M to 38.61M tokens and
LightRAG from 37.93M to 66.09M. QuALITY GraphRAG rises from 7.09M
to 7.49M. Other totals are unchanged. GraphRAG changes reflect retries. Ligh-
tRAG also retains earlier builds, making its cost sensitive to the accounting
boundary despite unchanged NarrativeQA rankings. We reuse saved predictions
throughout. Without request hashes, equal token counts cannot prove duplica-
tion. Unlogged calls remain unknown. We give cleaned, successful-before-cleanup
and all-recorded totals in the supplement.
    We summarize completed and unavailable checks in Table 5. A CI containing
zero leaves the direction uncertain. Establishing equal performance requires an
equivalence test.

graph/dataset pairs lack one or have a matching flat setting cheaper at ev-

forty with Q† ≈ 170. Yet flat twenty scores 24.8276 against RAPTOR’s 24.8314.
Both round to 24.8, concealing a difference of just 0.0038 points.

7.5    Evidence proxies and the score-based heuristic

We separate unresolved tests from successful ones in Table 6. NarrativeQA has
60.4% unresolved cases and a resolved-case median of two passages. Reading
marker 21 as an evidence size would turn a failed search into an apparent require-
ment. Our 18.5% exact agreement with annotated support count on multi-hop
questions further supports keeping these measures separate.
    Our heuristic predicts a MuSiQue GraphRAG threshold near 3.04, yet ob-
served graph-minus-flat margins are −9.8, +8.0 and +2.7 at support counts
two, three and four. The positive gap at three violates the strict threshold.
HippoRAG 2’s margins decrease: +12.5, +6.2, −5.4. On 2Wiki, GraphRAG’s
deficit grows with count. More support therefore fails to predict a larger graph

advantage. The supplement gives stratum sizes and paired intervals. These fail-
ures and our use of F1 as a probability proxy keep the heuristic exploratory.


8    Limitations and Threats to Validity
We examine limits from scope, implementation and interpretation.

Scope. We index all four corpora for each system but cover only 400 sampled
questions per multi-hop dataset, ten NarrativeQA documents and seventeen
QuALITY articles. NarrativeQA global has 100 questions versus 293 for other
runs. Our ranking covers one 8B generator, embedding model and chunking
scheme. Graph-specific tasks [19,20] and efficient indexes such as KET-RAG lie
outside this comparison. Dependent retrieval and context compression could use
the substantial unused flat allowance.

Engineering and resource boundaries. HippoRAG 2 uses a thread pool for fil-
tering and reading. Its diagnostic dense baseline replaces a failed class with
retrieval on the built index. LightRAG uses the server’s keyword schema, 64k-
token context and atomic state saves. Its MuSiQue rebuild retained cached re-
sponses. NarrativeQA was rebuilt cleanly, excluding calls before 30 September
2026, 09:10:17 UTC. GraphRAG and HippoRAG 2 also used cached restarts.
Estimated GraphRAG retries have equal input/output token counts 150 to
450 seconds apart and outputs over twenty tokens. This rule can merge distinct
requests. One to seven community reports per index failed to parse. Unlogged
failures remain unknown.

Interpretation. We count input/output tokens equally, excluding embedding and
reranking compute. Prompts, answer limits and extraction processing differ. Raw
scoring still uses cleanup. One annotator’s sparse judgments cannot establish
precise prevalence or agreement. Our heuristic’s fixed evidence set, independent
retrieval and constant graph success remain untested. Intervals condition on
saved runs without measuring index variability, limiting our conclusions to these
configurations.


9    Conclusion
With our 8B generator and sampled corpora, HippoRAG 2’s multi-hop ad-
vantage persists across flat settings. Our pipeline leads on NarrativeQA, while
QuALITY is inconclusive. Meaning changes occur in 21/76 outputs, with two
unclear cases. LightRAG’s MuSiQue gain depends on extraction, shifting RAP-
TOR’s cost intersection from ∼ 170 to 454 queries. We therefore verify original
answers and quality tolerances before assessing cost recovery.
